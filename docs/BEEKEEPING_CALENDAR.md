# Beekeeping Calendar — Buzzing Meadows

A real-world-accurate beekeeping calendar mapped to in-game seasons.
All tasks and events are grounded in actual apiary management practices.

---

## Overview

The in-game year is divided into **four seasons of 28 days each** (112 days total),
representing the real-world calendar from early spring through late winter.

Approximate real-world mapping for the **Temperate Midwest (Wisconsin)** baseline:
- Spring → Late March through May
- Summer → June through August
- Fall → September through October
- Winter → November through March

---

## 🌸 Spring (Days 1–28)

### What's Happening in the Hive
- The winter cluster breaks as temperatures warm above 50°F / 10°C.
- The queen resumes full laying in February/March; colony population begins rapid
  exponential growth (brood nest expanding outward from center).
- Bees begin foraging for early pollen (crocus, willow, maple) to feed larvae.
- Winter stores are being consumed rapidly as colony transitions from cluster to
  active colony.
- Varroa mite population begins reproductive cycle again as brood rearing ramps up.

### Player Tasks
- [ ] **First spring inspection**: assess winter survival, find queen or evidence
  of laying, check honey stores, look for disease
- [ ] **Reverse hive bodies** (if Langstroth): move empty boxes above the cluster
  to encourage upward expansion
- [ ] **Check for starvation**: if honey stores are low, feed 1:1 sugar syrup
  (simulates dilute nectar; stimulates brood rearing)
- [ ] **Assess varroa load**: perform alcohol wash or sugar roll (goal: <1% in spring)
- [ ] **Add honey supers** as nectar flow begins (usually when white clover blooms)
- [ ] **Watch for swarm cells** in frames 4–6 weeks after buildup begins
- [ ] **Order replacement equipment** or queens if needed

### Random Events
- 🌸 **Dandelion Bloom** (+10% nectar bonus for 3 days)
- ❄️ **Late Frost** (kills early foragers; -500 to -1,000 bees; pause foraging day)
- 🐝 **Swarm** (lose ~40% of population; gain a free swarm to catch or lose it)
- 🍀 **Early Clover Bloom** (triggers nectar flow early; add supers immediately!)
- 💀 **Winter Loss Discovered** (on Day 1 only: 15% chance colony didn't survive)

### Climate-Specific Variations

**Wisconsin (Temperate Midwest):**
- Spring arrives late (April); first inspection often mid-April
- Maple and willow are first pollen sources
- Late frosts possible through May; watch for cold snaps after early warm spells

**Pacific Northwest:**
- Wet, cool springs; bees may be flying but rainfall limits foraging
- Bigleaf maple provides massive early pollen load
- Watch for Nosema (intestinal pathogen) due to prolonged confinement

**Desert Southwest (Arizona):**
- Spring is the primary nectar flow (Feb–May), not summer
- Saguaro cactus and palo verde are major nectar sources
- Already planning for summer heat management

**Southeastern US:**
- Very early spring (February); tulip poplar is a major early flow
- Small hive beetles (SHB) begin reproducing in spring; add beetle traps

## ☀️ Summer (Days 29–56)

### What's Happening in the Hive
- Colony at peak population: 50,000–80,000 bees.
- Main nectar flow (white clover, basswood/linden, wildflowers) fills supers rapidly.
- Varroa mite population in **exponential growth phase** — maximum brood means
  maximum mite reproduction. Mite load may double every 3–4 weeks.
- Heat management becomes important; bees beard outside the hive on hot nights.
- Robbing behavior increases as nectar dearth hits midsummer.

### Player Tasks
- [ ] **Harvest honey** when supers are 80–90% capped
- [ ] **Add additional supers** to prevent swarm-triggering (space = less urge to swarm)
- [ ] **Monitor varroa load monthly** (alcohol wash target: <2% in summer)
- [ ] **Requeen** old queens (queens older than 2 years → reduced laying efficiency)
- [ ] **Watch for signs of robbing** (fighting at entrance → reduce entrance size)
- [ ] **Check for Small Hive Beetles** in southern climates

### Random Events
- 🌼 **Basswood/Linden Bloom** (major nectar flow event; fill supers fast)
- 🌵 **Nectar Dearth** (mid-July gap; no nectar → aggressive bees, robbing risk)
- ☠️ **Pesticide Event** (nearby crop spraying kills foragers; −2,000 bees)
- 🐝 **Robbing Attempt** (reduce entrance; if unchecked, colony may be robbed out)
- 🌡️ **Heat Wave** (bees focus on cooling; honey production pauses; risk of comb melt
  in unventilated Flow Hives)
- 👑 **Queen Supersedure** (colony quietly replaces aging queen; no intervention needed)

### Climate-Specific Variations

**Wisconsin:**
- White clover and basswood are major summer flows (late June–July)
- August often brings nectar dearth → varroa and robbing spike
- **Critical:** Begin varroa assessment in late July; plan fall treatment now

**Pacific Northwest:**
- Fireweed provides a spectacular late-summer flow (August)
- Moderate temperatures; less heat stress
- Varroa less severe than Midwest but still critical to treat

**Desert Southwest:**
- Summer is a dead period — extreme heat; no nectar; colonies may reduce population
- **Africanized Bee Risk**: defensive behavior, aggressive swarms; requeen with
  locally-bred European genetics
- Provide shade and water sources for colonies

**Southeastern US:**
- Summer nectar dearth common July–August; feed or harvest fall crop from goldenrod
- Small hive beetle populations peak; weak colonies vulnerable to SHB larvae damage
- Ventilation important: humidity management

## 🍂 Fall (Days 57–84)

### What's Happening in the Hive
- Population declining as queen reduces laying for winter transition.
- **Winter bees** being raised in late fall — these are physiologically different
  (fat bodies store protein for 6-month lifespan vs. summer bee's 6 weeks).
- Goldenrod and aster flows provide last nectar for winter stores.
- Varroa mite load is at its annual maximum — treating now protects winter bees
  from mite-vectored viruses (DWV = Deformed Wing Virus).
- Robbing pressure peaks as nectar dries up.

### Player Tasks
- [ ] **CRITICAL: Varroa treatment** by Day 65 (real-world: August 15 in Wisconsin)
  → Apivar (42 days) or Apiguard (28 days) before brood tapers off
- [ ] **Remove honey supers** before applying Apivar or Apiguard
- [ ] **Feed 2:1 sugar syrup** (heavy; simulates fall nectar; bees store it as
  winter food — they convert sucrose to honey-like stores using enzymes)
- [ ] **Reduce entrance** with entrance reducer (prevents mouse entry, reduces robbing)
- [ ] **Mouse guard** installation when temps drop below 50°F consistently
- [ ] **Assess winter stores**: hefting hive — light = too little; goal ≈ 60–80 lbs
  total weight in northern climates

### Random Events
- 🌻 **Goldenrod Flow** (Days 57–70; major nectar flow; good honey color + flavor)
- 🐀 **Mouse Invasion** (if entrance not reduced; mouse destroys comb over winter)
- 🐝 **Late Swarm** (unusual; colony may swarm in fall → very high fail risk)
- ☠️ **Varroa Critical Event** (if untreated and >4% load; brood health drops rapidly)
- 🟡 **Yellowjacket Robbing** (yellowjackets attack weak colonies; reduce entrance)
- 👑 **Queen Failure** (laying ceases; emergency queenright decision required)

### Climate-Specific Variations

**Wisconsin:**
- Treat varroa by **August 15** — this is the single most important date in the
  Wisconsin beekeeping calendar. Missing this window means winter bees are raised
  in high-mite conditions, dramatically reducing winter survival.
- Goldenrod (Aug–Sep) and aster (Sep–Oct) are critical late flows.
- Entrance reduction by October 1; mouse guards by October 15.

**Pacific Northwest:**
- Treat by September 1; winters are wet but not as cold
- Ivy provides late fall nectar (though invasive; bees use it)
- Watch for Varroa + Nosema combination in wet fall conditions

**Desert Southwest:**
- Mild fall; less urgency for winterization
- Second brood buildup in September/October when temps moderate
- May harvest fall honey crop

**Southeastern US:**
- Goldenrod flow important; treat varroa while brood still present (fall treatment)
- Watch for SHB population collapse timing — cool temps reduce beetle reproduction
- Light winterization; colonies rarely fully cluster

## ❄️ Winter (Days 85–112)

### What's Happening in the Hive
- Colony forms a **winter cluster** around the queen — a ball of bees that generates
  heat by shivering their flight muscles (no wing movement, just muscle vibration).
- The cluster moves upward through the hive slowly, following honey stores.
- **No brood** (or very little) in coldest months → ideal Oxalic Acid treatment window.
- Major mortality risk: **starvation** (consumed all stores before spring),
  **dampness** (condensation drips on cluster → fatal), **isolation starvation**
  (cluster cannot move to adjacent frames of honey when frozen).

### Player Tasks
- [ ] **Oxalic Acid dribble or vaporization** treatment (brood-free window → >97%
  mite kill; most effective varroa treatment available)
- [ ] **Heft test monthly**: lift hive from back corner; if very light → emergency
  fondant or candy board feeding (cannot feed liquid syrup in cold)
- [ ] **Upper entrance/ventilation**: add upper vent to allow moisture escape
  (condensation kills more colonies than cold does)
- [ ] **Insulation wrap** optional in northern climates (wind protection; does not
  need to be warm — bees generate own heat)
- [ ] **Plan spring**: order new queens, nucs, equipment; plan garden plantings
- [ ] **Record keeping**: document varroa counts, honey weights, treatment dates
  for next year

### Random Events
- ❄️ **Hard Freeze Snap** (extreme cold event; cluster must be large enough to survive)
- 🌡️ **Warm Spell** (January thaw → bees fly; deplete stores rapidly; cleansing flights)
- ⛄ **Starvation Event** (if stores below threshold; apply candy board immediately)
- 💧 **Dampness Event** (if ventilation not added; mold in hive; weakened bees)
- 🎉 **Winter Survival** (Day 112: colony successfully survived winter → major reward)
- 💀 **Winter Loss** (if conditions failed → dead colony discovered in spring)

### Climate-Specific Variations

**Wisconsin:**
- Cluster forms when temps consistently below 57°F (13°C), usually October
- Deep winter lows can reach -20°F (-29°C); proper insulation and windbreaks essential
- Oxalic acid treatment: December/January (brood-free window)
- Goal: ≥60 lbs of stored honey for a full-sized Langstroth colony

**Pacific Northwest:**
- Milder winters; cluster may break and rebee during warm spells
- Treat Oxalic Acid November–December during brood-free period
- Nosema risk elevated; Fumagilin-B treatment (where legal) or organic management

**Desert Southwest:**
- Barely any cluster; light winter management
- Risk: Africanized bee swarms during winter warm periods
- Minimal stores needed; bees may forage year-round

**Southeastern US:**
- Partial clustering; some brood throughout winter
- Treat Oxalic Acid November (brood minimum)
- SHB management continues; monitor for spring buildup timing

## Real-World Accuracy Notes

This calendar is grounded in resources from:
- **Extension Apiculture Programs**: University of Wisconsin-Madison, OSU, NC State
- **USDA AMS Honey Bee Health Coalition** — Varroa management guides
- **Bee Informed Partnership** — Annual colony loss survey data and treatment efficacy
- **Kim Flottum / Randy Oliver** — Practical beekeeping science communication
- **Marla Spivak (UMN)** — Hygienic behavior research and integrated pest management

Treatment timing windows follow **Integrated Pest Management (IPM)** best practices
as recommended by the Honey Bee Health Coalition's *Varroa Management Guide* (2023).
